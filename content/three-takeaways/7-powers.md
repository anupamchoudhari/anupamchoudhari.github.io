Most businesses that find something profitable get copied. A new kind of product works, competitors notice, prices fall, and within a few years the margins look like everyone else's. A few companies escape that pull and stay more profitable than their rivals for a decade or longer.

Hamilton Helmer's book is about what those few have in common. He calls it **power**: a condition that lets a business keep earning more than its competitors even after they have studied it closely. He argues there are only seven kinds, and that a strategy is simply a plan for getting at least one of them.

## The seven, in plain terms

| Power | What it means | A familiar example |
|---|---|---|
| Scale economies | Costs per customer fall as the business grows, so the biggest player can charge less and still earn more. | A streaming service spreads the cost of a big original series over every subscriber. Twice the subscribers, half the cost per head. |
| Network economies | The product gets more useful as more people use it. | A messaging app is only worth installing because everyone you know is already on it. |
| Counter-positioning | A newcomer picks a business model the market leader could copy, but won't, because copying it would hurt the leader's existing business. | Low-cost index funds. Established fund managers earned their fees from actively picked funds, so promoting a cheaper alternative meant shrinking their own revenue. |
| Switching costs | Leaving costs the customer more than staying. | The accounting system a company has run on for ten years, with every process and report built around it. |
| Branding | Customers pay more for the same thing because of what the name tells them. | The same silver necklace sells for several times more in a little blue box. |
| Cornered resource | Exclusive access to something valuable that others can't get. | A drug patent, or a team so good nobody else can hire it. |
| Process power | A way of working, built up over years, that rivals can see but can't reproduce. | Toyota published how its factories work. Competitors still took decades to match the results. |

## Three takeaways

**1. Every power has two halves, and plans usually describe only one.** The first half is a benefit: the thing makes money, saves money, or wins customers. The second half is a barrier: a reason a competitor can't simply do the same.

Most product plans are full of benefits. "This will improve retention" is a benefit. What is missing is the next line, the one that explains why a rival who sees the retention numbers won't build the same thing next quarter. When that line can't be written, the plan describes a good feature, not an advantage.

The less obvious point is that barriers are rarely built on purpose. A team builds an integration because one large customer asked for it, and two years later that integration is the main reason customers don't leave. The barrier was a side effect, and the useful skill is noticing it early enough to invest in it.

**2. Only some powers are available at the start.** Scale needs size. Network effects need users. Switching costs need customers who have settled in. Brand and process power take years. None of these can be the plan for a team that hasn't launched.

That leaves two that a newcomer can actually reach for: counter-positioning and a cornered resource. In practice, counter-positioning is the more common route, because it only needs a better business model and the patience to let the incumbent talk itself out of copying it. A lot of the companies that unseated an industry leader in the last twenty years did it this way.

**3. Timing matters more than it seems.** Power is usually won while a market is still changing: a new technology, a new customer behaviour, a regulation that moves the furniture. Once things settle, the positions are taken. That turns the usual worry, "are we too early?", into something closer to a requirement. Arriving after the market has settled usually means arriving after someone else has the barrier.

## Where I part ways

The book treats being better at execution, shipping faster and running a tighter operation, as something that doesn't count as power, because a competitor can always hire good people and catch up. Over a long enough period, that's right.

In AI products, though, the long period hasn't started yet. The models are rented from the same handful of providers. New techniques are published and copied within weeks. Most products in a category end up looking alike within a year. By the book's standard, almost no AI company has power today, and the honest advice would be to wait until something durable becomes available.

Nobody building a company can wait that long. What carries a team through that stretch is speed: shipping, learning from customers, and being in the room when a real barrier finally appears, whether that's a body of customer data, an integration nobody wants to rip out, or a workflow customers have built around the product. Speed isn't power, but it is what keeps a company alive long enough to get some.

## An earlier take

In 2024 I wrote about this book in a Medium article, [Balancing the Business Chakras](https://medium.com/@anupam.choudhari/balancing-the-business-chakras-a-holistic-approach-to-helmers-7-powers-d339853a17cf), which maps the seven powers onto the seven chakras through a fictional founder of an AI software company.
