When the road outside started getting a new footpath, I was genuinely happy. Footpaths in Bengaluru are usually more of a suggestion: a strip of broken slabs that ends without warning at a transformer, a parked car or a hole. This one was going to be wide, level and new.

Then the steel came up.

## Month one: the spikes

For about two months, a short length of rebar (the ribbed steel rod that goes inside concrete) stuck out of the ground every 20 metres or so, at roughly ankle height. They were anchors for light poles that hadn't arrived yet. Nothing marked them. No cones, no tape, no caps.

On a quiet street that would be a hazard. On a busy one, where people walk in clumps and watch the person in front of them rather than the ground, it was a trap. People tripped, and a few fell badly.

The fix didn't come from the contractor. It came from the street vendors, who, to be fair, occupy about half of this footpath themselves. After watching people stumble all day, they started pushing empty plastic water bottles onto the rods. A bottle on a spike is easy to see, and if you still walk into it, it's softer than steel.

It's a good fix. It's also the only safety review the project got, done for free by the people the footpath is officially supposed to be cleared of.

## Month two: lights, for the trees

The poles eventually went up. The lights came on about 15 days after that, which suggests that putting up a pole and connecting it to power are two separate jobs, done by two separate teams on two separate calendars. (That's an inference. Nobody publishes the schedule.)

The footpath was built around the existing trees, which is good. The poles turned out taller than the lower branches, which nobody seems to have checked. So most lamp heads now sit inside the canopy, and at night the street has a row of beautifully lit trees above a footpath that is still dark. The crew that came to fix the lamps had ladders and spanners. Nothing for cutting a branch, which probably belongs to yet another department.

## Month three: the cracks

Three months in, the new surface is already breaking up. Not from a truck or a digging crew, just from people walking on it and the occasional scooter. Something that took months to build is wearing out on a schedule you'd normally associate with a phone.

A footpath should be built to last 30 years. Concrete can do that. The real reason to aim for it is that the alternative is digging the whole thing up again every few years, which costs more, blocks the street again, and comes with a fresh round of spikes.

## Who writes the playbook?

My first guess was that nobody does. That's wrong. The Indian Roads Congress, the body that writes road standards for the whole country, published **IRC:103, *Guidelines for Pedestrian Facilities***, in 2012. It is specific, and it reads like it was written by someone who walks.

<div class="wide">

| | IRC:103 (India) | Typical US practice | What Bengaluru got |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kerb height above the road | 150 mm at most ([ITDP summary of the code](https://www.itdp.in/wp-content/uploads/2014/04/11.-Footpath-Design_Handout.pdf)) | 6 inches, about 150 mm ([US federal standard drawing](https://highways.dot.gov/federal-lands/design/plan-prep/cfl/cfl-template-drawings-fp24/sp609-A.pdf)) | A new footpath in CV Raman Nagar came out between 3.5 and 5 feet high ([The News Minute](https://www.thenewsminute.com/karnataka/bbmp-builds-new-footpath-bengaluru-its-too-high-humans-165179)) |
| Clear walking width | 1.8 m, so two wheelchairs can pass; 2.5 m in commercial areas | 36 inches minimum for an accessible route under the ADA | Roughly half, once vendors and parked scooters take their share |
| Clear height and obstructions | 2.2 m of headroom, with no poles, boxes, trees or signs in the walking zone ([Swarajya](https://swarajyamag.com/news-brief/delhi-footpaths-to-get-standardised-design-as-pwd-reviews-repair-norms)) | 80 inches of headroom under the ADA | Rebar at ankle height, for two months |
| Ramps at crossings | Kerb ramps for wheelchair users | No steeper than 1 in 12 ([US Access Board](https://www.access-board.gov/ada/guides/chapter-4-ramps-and-curb-ramps/)) | Sometimes. Then blocked with posts (see below) |

</div>

Look at the first row again. India's guideline asks for the same kerb height as an American street. 150 mm is 5.9 inches. The problem isn't the standard. It's that nothing ties the standard to the thing that gets built.

When Citizen Matters asked, engineers in the city's own road department [said Bengaluru didn't have a footpath guideline](https://citizenmatters.in/?p=9492) at all. Different zones of the city were [building to different designs](https://www.deccanherald.com/india/karnataka/bengaluru/while-bbmp-stays-busy-with-pothole-filling-footpaths-turn-into-deathtraps-948457.html), and the Deccan Herald found kerbs high enough that older people struggled to step up. Residents of the CV Raman Nagar stretch described getting onto it as climbing.

### The car door test

Every driver here knows what a tall kerb does. You park close to the footpath, open the door, and the bottom of it scrapes the kerb. Or worse, the door opens fine, you get in, and it won't close. Your weight has pushed the car down an inch, and the bottom edge of the door now sits below the top of the kerb.

In the US, between 2007 and 2011, I parked by rolling forward until the tyre touched the kerb. Rochester, New Mexico, and every state a road trip passed through: same height, every time, so you could park by feel. That is what a standard actually buys you. You stop having to think about it.

The ramps were just as consistent. The Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) requires a sloped ramp at crossings, no steeper than 1 in 12, so a 6-inch kerb gets a ramp about 6 feet long. Wheelchairs, strollers, suitcases and skateboards all use the same one.

## The ramp paradox

Here the Bengaluru version gets harder, and honestly it's the one part of this I don't have a clean answer for.

Build a gentle ramp for strollers, and it becomes an on-ramp for two-wheelers. The worst offenders aren't the motorbikes, which you can at least hear. It's the delivery riders on rented electric scooters, the ones bringing groceries in ten minutes. I like e-scooters. In traffic this loud, a silent vehicle is a small mercy. On a footpath, silent means the first you know of one is a handlebar brushing your elbow.

Bengaluru traffic police booked [12,032 cases of riding on footpaths](https://deccanherald.com/india/karnataka/bengaluru/bengaluru-crackdown-no-fix-for-footpath-riding-pedestrians-at-risk-4096222) in the first five months of 2026 alone.

So contractors put up bollards, the short posts that stop vehicles. Bollards stop strollers and wheelchairs too, so a gap gets left for wheelchair access, and that gap is [exactly wide enough for a scooter](https://deccanchronicle.com/nation/current-affairs/290318/bengaluru-revs-up-to-take-on-footpath-riders.html). Where the bollards are tight enough to work, the riders go back onto the road, often against the traffic. The behaviour doesn't change. It just moves somewhere more dangerous.

## What it costs, and what it costs not to

A quick conversion for anyone outside India: a lakh is 100,000 rupees, and a crore is 10 million. ₹10 crore is a little over a million US dollars.

Bengaluru's standard design for rebuilding a street properly is called Tender SURE: footpaths, cycle space and ducts for cables and pipes underneath, so nobody has to dig the footpath up again.

| What | Amount | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Tender SURE rebuild | About ₹10 to 12 crore per km. The 10.61 km around Majestic and KR Market was budgeted at ₹130 crore | [Deccan Herald](https://www.deccanherald.com/amp/story/india%2Fkarnataka%2Fbengaluru%2Froads-majestic-kr-market-get-2013962), [Citizen Matters](https://citizenmatters.in/tender-sure-roads-protesters-see-problems-with-process-cost) |
| Tender SURE "light" | About ₹3.7 crore per km, without the ducts (₹750 crore for 202 km across 100 stretches) | [The Quint](https://www.thequint.com/amp/story/news/india/qbengaluru-footpath-construction-child-marriage-bengaluru-lake) |
| Footpath upkeep, per ward | ₹25 lakh a year, in one city budget | [Citizen Matters](https://citizenmatters.in/?p=14287) |
| Fixing 15 wards | ₹93.17 crore for every footpath and junction, per a 2023 citizen audit priced at the city's own rates | [Deccan Herald](https://www.deccanherald.com/india/karnataka/bengaluru/bengaluru-needs-rs-9317-cr-for-walkable-footpaths-shows-audit-2661714) |

The per-km figures are my arithmetic from the project totals, not numbers the city publishes. The ₹25 lakh line is the one to sit with. That's about $28,000 a year to maintain every footpath in a ward of tens of thousands of people.

And the cost of not doing it: in 2023, Bengaluru recorded [292 pedestrian deaths](https://www.thenewsminute.com/karnataka/292-pedestrian-deaths-in-bengaluru-highest-in-2023-ncrb), the highest of the 53 Indian cities the national crime bureau tracks.

## Someone in India already solved this

The railways.

Indian Railways runs more than 60,000 km of track, in the same heat, the same monsoon and with the same budgets everyone else here works with. Its rulebook, the [Permanent Way Manual](https://indianrailways.gov.in/railwayboard/uploads/codesmanual/IRPWM/PermanentWayManualCh10_data.htm), says every stretch of track is inspected on foot, every day, by a keyman: one named person responsible for that beat. On top of that there are scheduled patrols after heavy rain, night patrols through the monsoon, and hot-weather patrols for welded rail, which can buckle. Findings get logged.

None of it is exotic. It's a person, a beat and a schedule, applied to something far more complicated than a footpath.

Instead, a footpath here belongs to everyone in slices. Since 2 September 2025, Bengaluru has been run by [five new city corporations under the Greater Bengaluru Authority](https://www.thenewsminute.com/karnataka/gba-to-replace-bbmp-from-sept-2-maheshwar-rao-will-be-new-commissioner), which replaced the old civic body, BBMP. The corporation's road engineers build the slab. The electricity company connects the lights. Someone else handles trees. Traffic police deal with riders and parking. Each slice has an owner. The walk itself doesn't.

When the city does act, it acts as a campaign. In July 2026 a ten-day "Safe Footpath" drive [cleared encroachments from 202.7 km of footpaths in three days](https://thesouthfirst.com/karnataka/safe-footpath-drive-clears-202-7-km-across-bengaluru/). That's real work. It's also a sign that nobody's job is to keep those kilometres clear in the weeks that follow, which is why [encroachments came back](https://www.deccanherald.com/india/karnataka/bengaluru/hc-orders-criminal-action-against-footpath-grabbers-977821.html) after the last court-ordered clean-up too.

### Asking for the records

India's Right to Information (RTI) Act lets anyone ask a public body for its records, for a ₹10 fee. Karnataka takes applications online at [rtionline.karnataka.gov.in](https://rtionline.karnataka.gov.in). For a footpath like this one, the useful questions are:

- the work order and drawings for the stretch, including the specified kerb height and width;
- the contractor's name and the defect liability period, which is how long they must repair defects at their own cost;
- the quality inspection reports, and who signed them;
- when power was requested for the new poles, and when it was connected.

The third one is the interesting answer. If someone signed off on this footpath, they either measured it or they didn't.

## What a fix would look like

*Turn the Ship Around* is David Marquet's account of taking command of the USS Santa Fe, a US nuclear submarine that was ranked near the bottom of its fleet. He'd spent a year preparing for a different submarine, so he didn't know this one well enough to run it by giving expert orders. Instead he flipped the model. The usual setup is **leader-follower**: the captain gives orders, the crew carries them out. Marquet moved to **leader-leader**: officers came to him saying "Captain, I intend to…", he asked questions until he was confident they'd thought it through, and then he said "Very well." Within about a year, the Santa Fe went from among the worst-rated boats in the fleet to among the best.

Footpath crews work in leader-follower mode, or more precisely leader-labourer. The drawing says an anchor goes here, so an anchor goes here. It's nobody's job to ask what happens to the person walking past it at 7pm for the next eight weeks.

The same crew in leader-leader mode sounds like this:

- "I intend to cap these rods with plastic sleeves today, since the poles aren't due for a month."
- "I intend to move this pole two metres along. The lamp will end up inside that tree."

Neither needs new money. Both need a crew that has been told the purpose (people walk here, including at night, including people who can't see well) and is allowed to act on it.

Marquet is careful to say that handing over control only works alongside two other things: competence, and clarity about the goal. Give a crew control without a clear standard and you get a different footpath on every street, which is pretty much what Bengaluru has now. The clarity half already exists. It's IRC:103, sitting in a PDF.

### If only one thing changes

A keyman for footpaths. One named person for every kilometre, with a phone number on a board at each end of the stretch, who walks it every week while it's being built and for years afterwards, and logs what they find somewhere the public can read.

The railways have shown this works with Indian budgets and Indian weather. It's also the one change that would have caught everything above: the spikes on day one, the lamp in the tree before the crew left, the first crack in week ten instead of month three. And the vendors could go back to selling things, instead of running quality control.
